2. ChatGPT — Best Overall AI Assistant for Students

If you want one general-purpose AI website to start with, ChatGPT is one of the most versatile choices.

It can help with:

  • Explaining difficult concepts
  • Summarizing information
  • Brainstorming
  • Writing and editing
  • Coding
  • Debugging
  • Working with uploaded files
  • Data analysis
  • Image-related tasks
  • Research using web search
  • Creating images
  • Learning through conversation

OpenAI's current Free tier includes access to features such as web search, file and image uploads, data analysis, image generation and GPTs, although individual tools have separate usage limits.

What students can use it for

ChatGPT works particularly well as a general study assistant. You can ask it to explain a difficult concept in simpler language, generate practice questions, review your answer, help debug code, or turn a large topic into a structured study plan.

You can also use it interactively instead of treating it like a simple search box. For example, you can ask it to quiz you, challenge your reasoning, or explain the same concept using a different approach.

Free plan

ChatGPT has a free tier with substantial access, but usage limits apply to individual tools and models. Advanced capabilities such as uploads, image generation and data analysis can have their own limits.

Paid plans provide higher access and additional capabilities. ChatGPT Plus is currently $20/month, while ChatGPT Pro is a higher-priced plan designed for users who need substantially more access.

Best for

General-purpose studying and productivity.

Limitations

  • AI-generated answers can be wrong.
  • Free access has usage limits.
  • It should not replace your own reasoning or course material.
  • A confident explanation is not automatically a verified explanation.

Our take

If you want to start with one general AI assistant, ChatGPT is a strong first choice. However, specialized tools can be better for particular tasks such as source-based research, mathematics, studying your own materials, or coding.

3. Google Gemini — Best for Students Already Using Google

Gemini is particularly convenient for students who already live inside Google's ecosystem.

It can be useful for:

  • Explaining concepts
  • Summarizing information
  • Brainstorming
  • Working with files
  • Writing
  • Research
  • Multimodal questions
  • Google-related workflows

Google's current Gemini Apps documentation says users without a Google AI plan receive standard limits, while paid Google AI plans provide higher access. Usage limits can depend on the model, feature, prompt complexity and conversation length.

The free experience also has a context window of 32K tokens according to Google's current plan comparison.

What students can use it for

Gemini can be useful when you're working with information that already exists in your Google workflow.

For example:

"Explain this topic, then give me five questions I should be able to answer before I consider myself ready."

Or:

"Turn these lecture notes into a revision plan."

The key advantage isn't that Gemini should replace every other AI tool. It's that it integrates naturally into Google's broader ecosystem.

Free plan

Gemini is available without a Google AI subscription with standard usage limits.

Google's paid AI plans currently include Google AI Plus, Pro and Ultra, with higher usage levels and additional features. The exact pricing depends on country and plan.

Best for

Students who already use Google services heavily.

Limitations

  • Free limits aren't simply a fixed "X prompts per day" number.
  • Some advanced features can require a paid plan.
  • Limits may change based on demand and system conditions.

Our take

Gemini is a strong second general-purpose AI tool, particularly if your academic workflow already revolves around Google.

4. Perplexity — Best for Research

Perplexity is different from a normal chatbot because its core experience is built around search and sourced answers.

This makes it particularly useful when you're researching a topic rather than simply asking an AI to explain something from its existing knowledge.

The current free Standard plan provides practically unlimited basic searches, a limited number of Pro Searches, limited file uploads and access to a system-selected model. Advanced models and image generation are not included in the free plan.

What students can use it for

Suppose you're researching:

"How has generative AI affected software development?"

A normal chatbot can provide an explanation.

Perplexity is more useful when your next question is:

"What are the sources behind these claims?"

That makes it useful for:

  • Research papers
  • Project research
  • Current technology topics
  • Comparing technologies
  • Finding primary sources
  • Exploring unfamiliar subjects

Free plan

$0.

Current published limits include:

  • Practically unlimited basic searches
  • 3 Pro Searches/day
  • 1 Research query/month
  • Limited file uploads
  • No advanced AI models on the free plan
  • No image generation on the free plan

Student advantage

Perplexity also has Education Pro for verified students and educators. It currently costs $10/month and adds features including Learn Mode, unlimited Pro Searches, file/image uploads and premium models.

Interestingly, Perplexity's Learn Mode is designed specifically around interactive learning rather than simply returning an answer.

Best for

Research and finding current, cited information.

Limitations

  • The free plan has strict limits on advanced research features.
  • Sources still need to be opened and evaluated.
  • A citation does not automatically mean the underlying claim is correct.
  • Perplexity is not necessarily the best tool for every writing or coding task.

Our take

For serious student research, Perplexity is one of the most useful tools on this list.

Use it to find and verify information—not simply to collect AI-generated answers.

5. Claude — Best for Long-Form Writing and Explanation

Claude is another general-purpose AI assistant, but it can be particularly useful for students who work with long explanations, writing and code.

Anthropic currently offers Claude Free at $0.

The free version supports:

  • Chat
  • Writing and editing
  • Code generation
  • Data visualization
  • Text and image analysis
  • Web search
  • Desktop access

What students can use it for

Claude can be useful when you want to turn complicated material into a clearer explanation.

For example:

"Here is my technical report. Identify the sections that are difficult to understand and suggest clearer wording without changing the technical meaning."

Or:

"Review this Java program and explain the design problems without rewriting the entire program."

This makes it useful as a reviewer, not just a generator.

Free plan

Claude Free costs $0, but free accounts have daily usage limits. Anthropic specifically notes that web search and page fetching contribute to those free usage limits.

Claude Pro is a paid upgrade; Anthropic currently lists it at $20/month in the US, with regional pricing where supported.

Best for

Writing, explanation, code review and thoughtful feedback.

Limitations

  • Free usage is limited.
  • Some advanced features belong to paid plans.
  • Like every generative AI system, it can make factual or reasoning errors.

Our take

You don't need both ChatGPT and Claude for every task.

But having access to both is useful when you want to compare explanations or get a second perspective on difficult material.

6. Gemini Notebook — Best for Studying Your Own Material

This one deserves special attention.

Google renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook in July 2026, while keeping it as a standalone research and learning product.

Its main strength is different from a general chatbot.

Instead of simply asking:

"Explain operating systems."

you can give it your own study material and ask questions about that material.

Gemini Notebook supports sources such as:

  • PDFs
  • Google Docs
  • Google Slides
  • Google Sheets
  • Word documents
  • Text
  • Markdown
  • CSV
  • PowerPoint
  • Images
  • Audio
  • Web URLs
  • YouTube URLs
  • ePub files

What students can use it for

Imagine you have:

  • Three lecture PDFs
  • Your professor's slides
  • A textbook chapter
  • Your own notes

You can place them into a notebook and ask questions based on those sources.

That is much more useful than repeatedly pasting pieces of the material into a normal chatbot.

Gemini Notebook can also generate things such as:

  • Summaries
  • Reports
  • Flashcards
  • Quizzes
  • Mind maps
  • Audio overviews

within its available limits.

Free plan

The current standard/free tier provides:

  • 100 notebooks/user
  • 50 sources/notebook
  • 50 chats/day
  • 3 Audio Overviews/day
  • 3 Video Overviews/day
  • 10 reports/day
  • 10 flashcard generations/day
  • 10 quizzes/day
  • 10 mind maps/day
  • 10 Deep Research uses/month

Individual sources can contain up to 500,000 words or 200 MB, and free notebooks support up to 50 sources.

Best for

Studying from your own notes, PDFs and course material.

Limitations

  • It works best when you provide good source material.
  • Free quotas still apply.
  • It isn't a replacement for understanding the original material.
  • Some advanced capabilities have higher limits on paid plans.

Our take

If you're a student who regularly studies from PDFs, lecture notes or slides, this may be the most specialized tool on the list.

7. Microsoft Copilot — Best for Web Answers and Microsoft Users

Microsoft Copilot is another general-purpose AI assistant, but its integration with Microsoft products makes it particularly relevant to students who use Windows, Edge or Microsoft 365.

Microsoft currently provides a free Copilot experience at no cost.

The free version can:

  • Answer questions
  • Help with writing
  • Brainstorm
  • Summarize
  • Use web search for real-time answers
  • Summarize webpages in Edge
  • Create images when signed in

What students can use it for

You can use Copilot for tasks such as:

"Explain this programming concept."

or:

"Summarize the main points of this webpage."

Its Edge integration can be particularly convenient when you're already reading something online.

Free plan

$0.

Microsoft says signing in with a personal account provides additional capabilities such as chat history, image creation, longer conversations and voice interactions.

Microsoft 365 subscriptions can provide additional Copilot capabilities inside Microsoft applications, but those are not part of the basic free Copilot experience.

Best for

Students already using Windows, Edge and Microsoft products.

Limitations

  • Advanced Microsoft 365 integration may require a paid subscription.
  • Feature limits vary by experience and account.
  • The free version isn't identical to Microsoft 365 Copilot.

Our take

You don't need Copilot simply because you're using Windows.

But if you're already working inside Microsoft's ecosystem, it's worth knowing how to use.

8. Wolfram|Alpha — Best for Mathematics and Computational Problems

Wolfram|Alpha is different from the general-purpose AI assistants above.

It describes itself as a computational knowledge engine, not a conventional search engine. Instead of simply returning webpages, it computes results using its underlying knowledgebase.

This makes it particularly valuable for students studying:

  • Mathematics
  • Physics
  • Engineering
  • Statistics
  • Quantitative subjects

What students can use it for

You can enter mathematical expressions and ask for computational results.

It is useful for checking things such as:

  • Algebra
  • Calculus
  • Equations
  • Functions
  • Statistics
  • Units
  • Mathematical properties
  • Quantitative data

Its strength is computation, not general conversation.

Free plan

Wolfram|Alpha is free for personal, noncommercial use.

A free Basic account provides customizable preferences, but step-by-step solutions are not included.

Wolfram|Alpha Pro currently starts at $9.99/month when billed monthly, or $5/month equivalent when billed annually, with step-by-step solutions, practice problems and other enhanced features.

There is also a dedicated student Pro plan.

Best for

Mathematics and computational verification.

Limitations

  • The free version does not provide all the step-by-step learning features.
  • It isn't a replacement for learning how to solve a problem yourself.
  • Some advanced features require Pro.

Our take

Don't use Wolfram|Alpha merely to copy answers.

Use it to check calculations, explore mathematical relationships and verify your work.

9. Canva — Best for Presentations and Visual Projects

Canva is primarily a visual design platform, but its AI features make it particularly useful for students creating:

  • Presentations
  • Posters
  • Infographics
  • Social graphics
  • Project visuals
  • Study materials
  • Resumes
  • Visual reports

Canva Free is available to anyone, including students. Canva also offers a separate education ecosystem depending on whether your institution provides access.

What students can use it for

Instead of asking AI to create a complete presentation and submitting it without understanding it, use Canva to accelerate the visual part of your work.

For example:

Research → write your content → organize the structure → use Canva to create the presentation → review every slide yourself.

Canva's AI tools include features such as Canva AI and Magic Write, although different AI tools belong to different access tiers.

Free AI access

Canva Free has a limited AI allowance.

Canva's current pricing information estimates that Free users can get up to 200 uses of Standard AI tools or up to 20 uses of Premium AI tools, depending on how the allowance is used. Ultra AI features are not included in Free.

The allowance resets monthly for Canva Free.

Best for

Presentations, visual assignments and design-heavy student work.

Limitations

  • Many premium templates and design elements remain paid.
  • AI usage is limited.
  • Not every Canva AI feature is available on Free.
  • AI-generated content still needs human review.

Our take

Canva isn't the tool you need to understand a difficult algorithm.

It's the tool you use when you already understand the content and need to communicate it effectively.

10. Grammarly — Best for Writing Improvement

Grammarly is particularly useful when the problem isn't:

"I don't know what to write."

but:

"I know what I want to say, but I want to say it more clearly."

Students can use it for:

  • Grammar
  • Spelling
  • Clarity
  • Tone
  • Writing feedback
  • Draft improvement
  • Basic AI-assisted writing

What students can use it for

Suppose you've written a project introduction.

Instead of asking an AI to rewrite everything, Grammarly can help identify:

  • Grammar problems
  • Awkward wording
  • Clarity issues
  • Tone problems

This makes it useful for:

  • Assignments
  • Reports
  • Emails
  • Resumes
  • Project documentation
  • Applications

Free plan

Grammarly Free costs $0/month.

It includes:

  • Grammar/spelling assistance
  • Writing tone feedback
  • 100 generative AI prompts/month

Grammarly Pro currently costs $12/month and raises the generative AI allowance to 2,000 prompts/month while adding features such as fuller rewriting and additional writing capabilities.

Best for

Improving writing you've already produced.

Limitations

  • The free AI allowance is limited to 100 prompts/month.
  • Advanced rewriting features are paid.
  • Grammarly cannot determine whether your academic argument is factually correct.
  • Good grammar does not automatically mean good academic writing.

Our take

Use Grammarly as an editor, not as the author of your assignments.

11. GitHub Copilot — Best for Students Who Code

If you're studying computer science, this is one tool worth knowing.

GitHub Copilot integrates with development environments such as VS Code, Visual Studio, JetBrains IDEs and Neovim.

It can help with:

  • Code completion
  • Explaining code
  • Debugging
  • Generating boilerplate
  • Refactoring
  • Understanding unfamiliar code
  • Learning programming patterns

What students can use it for

Imagine you're writing Java and don't understand why a particular method isn't working.

Instead of simply asking Copilot to rewrite the entire program, ask:

"Explain why this method returns the wrong result. Don't modify the code yet."

That turns Copilot into a learning assistant rather than an automatic code generator.

Free plan

GitHub Copilot Free currently includes:

  • 2,000 code completions/month
  • 50 chat requests/month
  • Access to selected models
  • Copilot CLI
  • Community support

There is something particularly important for students:

Verified students can receive Copilot Student for free , with substantially higher access than the normal Free plan. GitHub says student eligibility is reevaluated monthly.

Best for

Programming students and developers.

Limitations

  • AI-generated code can contain bugs or insecure approaches.
  • Students can become dependent on autocomplete if they don't practice independently.
  • Free usage is limited.
  • You need to understand the code you accept.

Our take

For a computer science student, Copilot is most useful when it accelerates your thinking rather than replacing it.

14. A Better Student AI Workflow

The biggest mistake is opening an AI website and immediately asking:

"Do my assignment."

A better workflow is:

Step 1 — Understand

Use ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude to understand the concept.

↓

Step 2 — Research

Use Perplexity to find current information and sources.

↓

Step 3 — Study your material

Use Gemini Notebook when your own PDFs, notes or slides are the primary source.

↓

Step 4 — Practice

Try solving the problem yourself.

Use Wolfram|Alpha or an AI assistant to check your reasoning where appropriate.

↓

Step 5 — Build

For programming, use GitHub Copilot as an assistant while keeping control of the code.

↓

Step 6 — Create

Use Canva for presentations, diagrams and visual communication.

↓

Step 7 — Edit

Use Grammarly to improve writing you've already produced.

This workflow is much more useful than having ten AI tabs open and asking all of them the same question.

15. What "Free" Actually Means

This is important enough to repeat.

When we call these websites free, we mean:

You can use a meaningful version of the service without paying for a subscription.

It does not mean:

Unlimited access to every feature.

For example:

  • ChatGPT limits some tools and advanced usage.
  • Gemini uses usage limits that can change based on demand and features.
  • Perplexity limits advanced searches on Free.
  • Claude has daily free usage limits.
  • Gemini Notebook has daily and monthly quotas.
  • Canva uses a monthly AI allowance.
  • Grammarly provides 100 generative AI prompts/month on Free.
  • GitHub Copilot Free has specific completion and chat quotas.
  • Wolfram|Alpha's advanced step-by-step features are part of Pro.

So don't choose a tool simply because its homepage says "Free."

Look at what is actually included in the free tier.

16. Alternatives & Comparisons

There are many other AI websites that could have appeared on this list.

Some are excellent, but we deliberately didn't include every popular tool.

Gamma

Gamma can be useful for AI-assisted presentations and documents. Its Free plan currently provides AI creation and up to 10 slides in a single prompt, but its initial AI credits do not refresh on the Free plan.

It's a reasonable alternative to Canva for presentation-heavy work.

Other AI coding assistants

There are many alternatives to GitHub Copilot.

But for students already learning software development, GitHub's integration with popular IDEs and its verified student benefit make it particularly practical.

Traditional search engines

Don't completely replace Google or other search engines with AI.

Traditional search remains useful when:

  • You know the exact documentation you need.
  • You're looking for an official page.
  • You need to compare multiple primary sources.
  • You want to verify an AI-generated claim.

The strongest student workflow combines AI + search + primary sources, rather than treating any one system as an unquestionable authority.

17. Pros & Limitations

✅ Pros

  • Most tools provide genuinely useful free access.
  • Different tools specialize in different academic tasks.
  • Students can combine them without paying for every service.
  • Several tools have particularly useful student-focused features.
  • They can reduce time spent on repetitive tasks.
  • They can make difficult material easier to explore interactively.

⚠️ Limitations

  • Free limits can change.
  • AI answers can be wrong.
  • Citations still need verification.
  • Students can become dependent on AI.
  • Some "free" features are deliberately limited.
  • Premium models and features often remain behind subscriptions.
  • AI should support learning rather than replace the student's own work.

18. Frequently Asked Questions

Are these AI websites actually free?

Yes. Each website listed here offers a usable free tier or free access.

However, free does not mean unlimited.

Several have quotas, model restrictions or feature limitations.

Which is the best free AI website for students?

If you want one general-purpose tool, ChatGPT is a strong starting point.

But there isn't one tool that wins every category.

For research, Perplexity may be more appropriate.

For source-based studying, Gemini Notebook may be better.

For mathematics, Wolfram|Alpha is more specialized.

For coding, GitHub Copilot is more relevant.

Can I use all ten without paying?

Yes, you can access free versions of all ten.

However, you will encounter different limitations depending on the service.

You also don't need to use all ten.

A small set of tools that fits your workflow is usually better.

Which free AI is best for research?

Perplexity is one of the strongest choices because its core experience is designed around web search and citations.

But don't blindly trust its answer.

Open the important sources and verify the claims.

Which AI is best for studying from PDFs?

Gemini Notebook is particularly well suited to this because it is designed around user-provided sources.

Its free tier currently supports up to 50 sources per notebook.

Which AI is best for mathematics?

Wolfram|Alpha is an excellent computational tool.

However, its free version doesn't include the full step-by-step experience available in Pro.

Is GitHub Copilot free for students?

Yes, but there are two different situations.

The normal Copilot Free plan has limited usage.

Verified students can qualify for Copilot Student at no cost, which provides substantially greater access.

Is Canva AI completely free?

No.

Canva itself has a Free plan, and some AI features are available on Free, but Canva also has Premium and Ultra AI features that require paid access. Free users also have a limited monthly AI allowance.

Can I use AI to write my assignments?

Technically, AI can generate assignment text.

But that's not necessarily a good learning strategy.

A better approach is:

Understand → research → create your own answer → use AI to review → verify → submit your own work.

Also follow your institution's rules regarding AI-assisted academic work.

Should I pay for an AI subscription as a student?

Not automatically.

Start with free plans.

Pay only when you repeatedly encounter a limitation that genuinely affects your work.

For example, if you regularly hit the free research limit, a paid research plan may make sense.

If you rarely reach the limit, paying every month may provide little additional value.

Do I need ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude simultaneously?

No.

They're overlapping general-purpose assistants.

You might keep more than one because they sometimes provide different explanations or perform differently on particular tasks, but you don't need to send every question to all three.

Can AI replace textbooks and professors?

No.

AI is useful for explanations, practice, brainstorming and exploration.

Your course material, textbooks, instructors, documentation and primary sources remain important sources of knowledge.

19. Final Verdict

The best free AI websites for students aren't necessarily the ones with the biggest feature lists.

They're the ones that solve specific student problems well.

If we had to reduce this entire article to a practical toolkit, we'd start with:

🥇 General learning

ChatGPT

🔎 Research

Perplexity

📚 Study from your own material

Gemini Notebook

🧮 Mathematics

Wolfram|Alpha

💻 Programming

GitHub Copilot

🎨 Presentations and visual work

Canva

✍️ Writing improvement

Grammarly

Then keep Gemini, Claude and Copilot available as alternative general-purpose assistants when a different approach is useful.

The important lesson isn't to collect AI websites.

It's to learn which tool to use for which problem.

A student who knows ten AI tools but asks all of them to do their homework isn't necessarily getting much value.

A student who knows:

ChatGPT for understanding → Perplexity for research → Gemini Notebook for course material → Wolfram|Alpha for computation → Copilot for coding → Canva for presentation → Grammarly for editing

has a much more useful system.

And that's ultimately what makes an AI tool valuable: not how impressive its feature list looks, but whether it helps you learn, create and work more effectively without taking control of the learning process away from you.
